The Dog Park Project

Eight virtual machines. A fictional business to run. One week of pen-testers from 4620 trying to take you offline. This guide helps you think your way through it — it will not hand you the answers.

Part 1

The mission

You are an IT team standing up a new company from nothing. By the start of pen-test week, orange-team "customers" will visit your website and place real orders. The 4620 class will simultaneously try to take you offline — exploiting weak rules, dropping your services, pivoting through whatever you forgot to lock down. Your grade is your uptime.

What "done" looks like

  • A reachable website that lets orange-team customers buy your 3 goods and 3 services.
  • An internal team — admins, users, guests — that can log into any workstation with one set of credentials.
  • Email between staff. A database backing orders. A monitoring dashboard you actually watch.
  • A firewall that says "no" by default and "yes" only to the traffic the business needs.
  • Documentation good enough that a teammate out sick on Monday can fix things on Tuesday.

Every other task on the requirements sheet — DHCP, routes, AD, chatbot, backups — exists to support those five outcomes. When in doubt, ask: does this make the storefront work or keep it up?

Why most teams stall in week 1 They start by configuring individual machines without a picture of the whole network in their heads. Two days later their RHEL client can't ping the firewall and nobody knows whether the cable, the route, the firewall rule, or DNS is the problem. Read the next two sections before you touch a VM.

Your eight machines

Before anything else, learn what each machine is for and which zone it lives in. Every config decision you make later flows from this. Click each row to open the brief.

[ Internet ] | WAN (172.31.0.0/24) | [ Vyatta WAN ] 172.31.0.1 | [ Firewall ] 192.168.2.2 / \ DMZ / Screened LAN (192.168.1.0/24) (10.0.1.0/24) | / \ [ Vyatta LAN ] / \ 192.168.1.1 [ Win Web ] [ RHEL DB ] | 10.0.1.100 10.0.1.200 +---------+---------+---------+ | | | | [AD/DNS] [RHEL cli] [Kali] ... .5 .100 .50
Exact addresses are illustrative — your team's instructor-issued plan is the source of truth. The point is that three zones exist and each machine sits in exactly one of them.

Role: the gatekeeper between every zone. Every packet that crosses zones passes through here. If two machines in different zones can't reach each other, this is your first suspect — and the first place you should snapshot before changing anything.

Mental model: default-deny. Start from "nothing talks to anything" and add only the conversations the business needs. A rule that says "allow LAN to internet" is broader than "allow LAN clients to web on TCP 80/443" — and the broader rule will haunt you on pen-test week.

Role: the edge router that connects you to the outside world. It is the last hop before "the internet" and the first hop a return packet sees coming back. It usually does NAT — translating your private addresses to the single public-ish address on the WAN.

Key question to answer: what is its WAN-side address, what is its LAN-side address (facing the firewall), and what default route does it use to reach the internet?

Role: routes between the firewall and your internal LAN switch. Your internal machines use the Vyatta LAN as their default gateway — when an internal client wants to reach the web server in the DMZ, it sends the packet here first.

Why two routers? Real enterprises put one router at the edge and another deep inside so you can apply different policy at each layer. It also gives you a clean place to terminate VLANs and DHCP scopes inside the LAN.

Role: the identity backbone. Active Directory holds your users, groups, and computer accounts. DNS — running on the same box — is how every other machine finds it. DNS comes before identity, and identity comes before almost everything else.

Watch for: machines that point at the wrong DNS server. A workstation that uses Google DNS (8.8.8.8) instead of your AD/DNS box cannot find dc01.yourcompany.local — so it cannot join the domain, no matter how many times you click "Join."

Role: hosts the customer-facing site that takes orders from the orange team. Lives in the screened subnet because it must be reachable from the outside, but you don't want an attacker who pops it to pivot straight into your LAN.

Will need: a route to the database (DMZ → DMZ), a route out to the internet for software updates, and a firewall rule that allows the orange team to reach TCP 80/443. It must not be allowed to freely reach into the LAN.

Role: backs the web server. Stores goods, services, customers, orders. It is the most valuable target on your network — if 4620 corrupts or drops it, your business is done.

Think about: who is allowed to talk to it? Only the web server, on the database port — not the whole DMZ, not the LAN, definitely not the internet. Backups are not optional and a backup you have never restored is not a backup.

Role: a Linux workstation for staff. Has to authenticate against AD just like the Windows clients do — which means installing the right packages (realmd, sssd, oddjob, adcli, samba-common-tools), pointing at your AD/DNS server, and joining the realm.

Common failure: the team gets Windows joined first, declares victory, then can't figure out why RHEL won't join. The fix is almost always DNS — see Part 5.

Role for 4910: your blue-team self-test box. Use it to scan your own network with nmap and confirm that the only open ports are the ones you intended. If Kali sees more than you expected, 4620 will see it too.

What it is not: a place to launch attacks at other teams. 4620 attacks you; you scan yourselves.

Part 3

The 4910 mindset

If you only take four things from this guide, take these. They are worth more than any specific config trick because they apply to every problem you will hit.

① Snapshot before you change

Proxmox snapshots are the difference between "I broke routing for 20 minutes" and "we lost a day rebuilding." Before you touch the firewall, the routers, or the AD server, take a snapshot. Name it for what you are about to try.

Habit: snapshot → change one thing → test → if it works, snapshot again and move on. If it doesn't, roll back and re-read.

② Isolate one variable

When something doesn't work, do not change three settings and reboot. Change one thing, test that one thing, then change the next. A broken network with one wrong setting and a broken network with three wrong settings look identical — but only one is fixable in an hour.

③ Read the error

Most students reading this lose more time to guessing than to anything else. The terminal is telling you what's wrong. Paste the exact message into your AI IDE chat or into a search engine before you change a config. Name or service not known means something very different than No route to host.

④ Document while it's fresh

When you finally get the firewall talking to the DMZ, the steps are crystal clear. Three days later, after AD has eaten your Tuesday, you will not remember. Write it down now — in the team Google Doc — including the things you tried that didn't work.

The single most common 4910 time sink Teams spend hours fighting connectivity because they jumped to the firewall before confirming the routers were healthy, or jumped to AD before confirming DNS resolved. Walk the network bottom-up: link → IP → gateway → route → firewall → DNS → service. If a step below fails, every step above will also fail — but for misleading reasons.
Part 4 · deep dive

Connectivity: DHCP, routing, and the three zones

Most teams blow a week here. This section is long on purpose. The goal is not to give you commands — it is to make you understand the questions you should be answering before you type anything.

Three zones, three address spaces

Your network is split into three trust zones. Each has its own address space. A machine's address tells you which zone it lives in, who its default gateway is, and which router route table it depends on.

WAN

172.31.0.0/24
  • Edge of the network
  • Vyatta WAN lives here
  • Talks to the "internet"
  • NAT happens here

DMZ / Screened

10.0.1.0/24
  • Public-facing services
  • Web server, database
  • Reachable from internet
  • Cannot freely reach LAN

LAN

192.168.1.0/24
  • Trusted internal network
  • Workstations, AD/DNS, Kali
  • Vyatta LAN is gateway
  • Reaches DMZ via firewall

DHCP or static? — the decision exercise

Before you stand up a DHCP scope, decide which machines get static addresses and which ones get leases. There is a real principle here, not just preference.

Q: Should the AD/DNS server use DHCP?

Think about it before reading on. If a server changes address, what breaks?

No. Anything that has clients pointing at it by IP, or that other machines look up by name, must hold a stable address. Servers (AD/DNS, web, DB, mail) get static addresses every time. Document them.

Q: Should the RHEL client workstation use DHCP?

What is gained or lost by handing it a dynamic address?

Yes — usually. Workstations are interchangeable. DHCP saves you from configuring each one by hand and is exactly what real enterprises do. Reserve a chunk of the LAN scope (say, the high end) for staff laptops and let DHCP hand them out.

Q: Should the routers and firewall use DHCP?

What is the gateway for the LAN clients?

Never. The router's address is what every other machine points at as its default gateway. If the router's address moved, the entire LAN would lose its way out.

So the rule of thumb you should be able to defend in your write-up:

Static IPs for infrastructure (routers, firewall, AD/DNS, web, DB, mail, monitoring). DHCP for workstations. Every static address goes into your team Google Doc the same minute you assign it.

The four things every DHCP scope must hand out

When you configure DHCP on the Vyatta LAN (or wherever your scope lives), the lease is not just an address. It is four pieces of information without which the client cannot function:

  1. IP address + subnet mask — tells the client which other machines are "local".
  2. Default gateway — where to send any packet not destined for the local subnet. For your LAN, this is the Vyatta LAN's LAN-side address.
  3. DNS server — the AD/DNS box, by IP. If this is wrong, name lookups fail and joining the domain is impossible.
  4. Lease time — how long the lease is valid. The default is usually fine.

A client that has an IP but no gateway can ping its own subnet and nothing else. A client that has a gateway but wrong DNS can ping the firewall by IP but cannot resolve a name. The symptom tells you which piece is missing.

Routing: making packets find their way

Two routers, three zones. Each router needs to know how to reach the zones it doesn't directly own. The mental shortcut:

RouterKnows directlyNeeds a route toVia
Vyatta WANWAN (172.31.0.0/24)everything internalthe firewall
FirewallWAN side + DMZ + LAN-sidenothing — it is the hub—
Vyatta LANLAN (192.168.1.0/24)DMZ + internetthe firewall

Notice that both Vyattas point at the firewall for anything they don't own. The firewall is the natural hub of your routing universe because it already sits between every pair of zones. Don't configure routes that try to bypass the firewall — that defeats the whole point of having one.

NAT lives on the WAN router Your internal addresses (10.0.1.x, 192.168.1.x) are private and cannot appear on the internet. The Vyatta WAN translates them to its public-side address on the way out. If internal machines can reach the WAN router but not "the internet," NAT is the first place to check.

The ping ladder — your zone-by-zone diagnostic

When connectivity breaks, do not start by inspecting firewall rules. Walk this ladder from the bottom up. The first rung that fails is where the problem actually lives.

1
ping 127.0.0.1
Does this machine's network stack work at all? If not, it's a VM-level problem, not a network one.
2
ping <own IP>
Does the interface have an address? If ip addr shows nothing, DHCP didn't lease — go fix DHCP before anything else.
3
ping <default gateway>
Can you reach the next-hop router on your own subnet? If not, you have a Layer 2 / cabling / VLAN problem on Proxmox.
4
ping <firewall inside address>
Can you cross to the firewall? If not, the LAN router is not forwarding — check its route table.
5
ping <web server in DMZ by IP>
Can you cross zones? If steps 1–4 pass but this fails, you are almost certainly looking at a firewall rule.
6
ping 8.8.8.8
Can you reach the internet by IP? If not, the WAN router or NAT is broken. (8.8.8.8 is just a famous IP — any external one works.)
7
ping google.com
Can you reach the internet by name? If 6 works but 7 fails, your DNS is wrong. Do not blame routing.
Why the ladder works Each rung depends on every rung below it. If rung 5 fails but rung 4 passes, you have just proved that links, addressing, and routing on this side are fine — the problem must be at the boundary you just crossed. Stop guessing. Look at the boundary.

Part 5

Firewall rules: writing the conversation list

Most teams treat the firewall as a tangle of rules to add until things work. That is how you end up with an open perimeter and a 4620 team that eats your lunch. The professional approach is to write down the conversations your business actually needs first, then translate each conversation into a rule.

Step 1 — list the conversations

Before opening the firewall UI, write a table like this in your team doc. Fill it in for your specific business. The columns matter:

From (zone)To (zone)ServiceWhy the business needs it
InternetDMZHTTPS/HTTP (443/80)Orange-team customers reach the storefront
Web (DMZ)DB (DMZ)your DB portStorefront queries products and writes orders
LANInternetHTTPS (443)Staff browsing for updates, docs, packages
LANDMZHTTPS/SSH (443/22)Admins manage the web server
LANAD/DNS (LAN)DNS, Kerberos, LDAP, SMBIdentity and name resolution for everything
DMZLANnothing by defaultIf the web server is popped, the attacker should be stuck in the DMZ

Step 2 — default-deny posture

Set your firewall so that anything not in the conversation list above is denied. Then add allow rules one row at a time. This is harder than the opposite ("default-allow and block what looks bad") but it is the only posture that survives a real attacker.

The trap of "allow all, fix later" Every team is tempted to open everything up "just for now, until things work." Pen-test week arrives, the rules never get tightened, and 4620 walks in through the side door. Tighten as you go. An allow rule with a specific port and direction takes the same minute as a sloppy one — and is the difference between a graded uptime and a graded outage.

Step 3 — read what each rule actually says

When you write a rule, narrate it back to yourself in plain English: "Allow traffic from any address, to the web server's DMZ address, on TCP 80 and 443." If you cannot narrate it, you do not understand it, and you cannot defend it during pen-test week.

Part 6 · deep dive

Identity: AD, DNS, and joining the domain

This is the other section where teams lose the most time. The fixes are almost never about Active Directory itself — they are about the silent dependency underneath it: DNS.

The order matters: DNS → AD → join

Active Directory is a service. Like every service, clients have to find it before they can talk to it. They find it by looking up the domain controller's name in DNS. If DNS gives them the wrong answer (or no answer), nothing else you do will help.

1
Stand up the AD/DNS server with a static IP
Both AD and DNS roles live on the same box. The IP is now the most important number on your network — document it.
2
Promote the server to a domain controller and create the domain
Pick a domain name you will not regret (something.local is common). This name is what every other machine will join.
3
Hand out the AD/DNS server's IP as the DNS server in DHCP
Every client must learn about this box from DHCP, or be configured statically to point at it. Not 8.8.8.8. Not the router. The AD/DNS box.
4
Verify name resolution from a client
From a workstation, look up the domain name. If it returns the AD/DNS box's IP, you are clear to join. If it returns nothing or NXDOMAIN, stop — fix DNS first.
5
Join the client to the domain
Now the join will work, because the client can actually find the controller it is supposed to join.

Windows clients — the short version

On a Windows workstation:

  • Set the DNS server to your AD/DNS box (in the NIC properties, or via DHCP).
  • From a command prompt, look up your domain: nslookup yourcompany.local. You should see the AD/DNS server's IP. If not, fix DNS before going further.
  • System Properties → Change settings → Domain → enter your domain name → authenticate with a domain admin → reboot.
  • On next login, sign in as YOURCOMPANY\username with the domain user's password.
The classic Windows join failure "An Active Directory Domain Controller (AD DC) for the domain ... could not be contacted." Nine times out of ten this means the client's DNS server is wrong. It is asking 8.8.8.8 or its router about yourcompany.local — and neither of them have heard of it. Fix DNS and the join works.

RHEL clients — what the tools actually do

Linux domain join uses a stack of small tools that each do one thing. Knowing what each one does makes troubleshooting tractable instead of magical.

ToolWhat it does
realmd The high-level "join the domain" command. It orchestrates the other tools.
adcli The actual AD-join worker that realmd calls. Talks to Kerberos and creates the computer account in AD.
sssd The daemon that runs after the join. Handles login, group lookups, caching credentials. If logins fail post-join, this is who to look at.
oddjob + oddjob-mkhomedir Creates the user's home directory on first login. Without these, the user joins but has nowhere to land.
samba-common-tools Provides Kerberos / Samba helpers realmd uses under the hood.

The flow looks like this, conceptually:

  1. Install the packages above.
  2. Confirm the host's /etc/resolv.conf points at the AD/DNS server (or that NetworkManager set it from DHCP). Test with realm discover yourcompany.local — it should print details about your domain. If it can't discover, your DNS is wrong.
  3. Join: realm join yourcompany.local -U administrator. It prompts for the AD admin password and creates a computer account in AD.
  4. Configure sssd to allow home-directory creation and to format the username the way you want (with or without the domain prefix).
  5. Test: id administrator@yourcompany.local should return the user's UID, GID, and groups. Now su - administrator@yourcompany.local or ssh as a domain user.
RHEL join symptoms and what they mean
  • realm discover returns nothing → your resolv.conf is wrong. Fix DNS before anything else.
  • realm join fails with "Insufficient permissions" → you didn't authenticate as a domain admin (or the account is locked out).
  • Join succeeds but id user@domain returns "no such user" → sssd isn't running, or its config is wrong. systemctl status sssd.
  • You can log in but have no home directory → oddjob-mkhomedir isn't enabled or PAM doesn't call it. Look at authselect.

The single rule that prevents 80% of identity pain

Confirm DNS resolves the domain name from the client, every single time, before you click "join." If nslookup on Windows or realm discover on Linux can't see the domain, the join cannot succeed and no amount of clicking will change that.

Part 7

The business: website, orders, and the orange team

Everything you just built exists so a customer can place an order. During pen-test week, the orange team plays the role of those customers — they will visit your site, click around, try to buy your three goods and three services, and email you about problems. Your business is graded on whether they can.

Tie every config decision back to the storefront

Use this as a sanity check whenever you are about to spend an hour on something:

  • If it makes the storefront work or stay up: do it.
  • If it is required by the task sheet but doesn't touch the storefront: do it, but don't let it block the storefront.
  • If it's a cool feature nobody asked for: after pen-test week.

Use the AI IDE for the site itself

You don't need to write the storefront by hand. Open Cursor or Antigravity, describe your business in a PRD, and iterate. The "Vibe Coding" workflow your instructor outlined in the Dog Park doc gets you a working site faster than any of you will by hand.

But — keep the website's secrets in .env (API keys, DB passwords, the Cyberlab GPU bearer token). Push the code to GitHub; do not push the .env. 4620 reads GitHub.

Orange-team customers are real users

During pen-test week, your support inbox is real. Someone is going to email saying "I can't check out." Have a process: a triage rotation, a templated reply, a way to log the issue. The team that wins pen-test week is the team whose customers feel served, not the team with the prettiest infrastructure diagram.

Part 8

Pen-test week: when 4620 arrives

The 4620 class will try to take you offline, exfiltrate your database, deface your site, and otherwise prevent you from doing business. They are graded on impact. You are graded on uptime. This is a real fight.

Watch, don't wait

The monitoring dashboard you built in Section 9 of the task sheet is not for show. Put it on a screen in the team's workspace. The first sign of trouble will usually be a metric, not a 4620 student knocking on the door.

Snapshots are your panic button

If a server gets defaced or destroyed, do not try to clean it. Roll back to the most recent clean snapshot, identify how 4620 got in, close that door, and rebuild from a known-good state. Faster every time.

Have a credentials playbook

When 4620 makes noise, the first instinct is to rotate passwords. Decide now: where are the credentials stored, who has the authority to rotate them, and how do you get the new ones to staff without using the channel that may already be compromised?

Take notes during the week

Every attack and every defense is graded material. Have someone whose job for the week is documenting what 4620 tried, what worked, what didn't, and what you fixed. The post- mortem is half your grade.

The mindset shift Before pen-test week, your goal is to build. During pen-test week, your goal is to operate. Stop adding features. Stop refactoring. Watch the dashboard, take orders, rotate when you must, and document everything.

One last thing

If your team is stuck and not sure what to do next, do this in order:

  1. Snapshot.
  2. Walk the ping ladder from the broken machine. Whichever rung fails is your real problem.
  3. Read the error — don't summarize it, copy-paste it. Into your AI IDE, into Google, into the team channel.
  4. Change one thing and test again. Then snapshot the win and keep going.

Build the business. Defend it. Ship orders. Good luck.
